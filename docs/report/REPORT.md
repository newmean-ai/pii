## Abstract

Survey tools, support chats and AI assistants receive text that often contains names, phone numbers, addresses and identity
numbers. Masking the detected spans **in the browser, before the text is sent**, keeps them off the server — whatever the
detector misses is still sent — but needs a detector that is small enough to download and fast enough to run on a phone, in
many languages. Public detectors are either large or centred on English and Latin-script data. We describe data v1.0: 1,156,505
synthetic text items (1.07M for training, plus validation and two test sets) in 54 languages, with per-country identifier formats,
realistic and foreign names, ~75 subtypes and two **PII levels** (a masking policy separating direct from indirect
identifiers), generated by three Claude models with every cost reported. In a blind review of 300 random validation rows by
two model families, 2.7% of the rows that enter training had labels that both reviewers changed. Four browser students of 2.6–15.5 MB
distilled from two private teachers miss 1.6–3.3% of PII characters on the reviewed 54-language public test of newmean-pii-dataset, our synthetic data, against
19.6–75.3% for eight public detectors and a regex baseline (most of which follow other label policies), and 7.6–9.8% on the
Korean KDPII corpus, which none of our models saw. The models, the SDK and the public test of newmean-pii-dataset are released, with a leaderboard; the training data is available
on request.

## 1. Introduction

Personal data enters web forms and chat boxes all the time: a survey respondent mentions a colleague by name, a customer pastes
a bank account into a support chat, a user pastes a configuration file with an API key into an AI assistant. Once the text
reaches a server, it is logged, cached and processed by third parties. Masking the text **on the device, before it is sent**,
keeps the masked parts from reaching the server; anything the detector misses is still sent. This is the design principle behind
newmean.ai's research survey tool, which masks the detected personal data in the respondent's browser and only then submits the
answers, and behind the SDK (`@newmean-ai/pii`) that this report's models are for.

A browser-side detector must be **small** (a few megabytes over a mobile connection), **fast** (tens of milliseconds per
message on a phone) and **multilingual and multi-domain** (survey answers, HR forms, medical notes, chat, logs, OCR text).
Existing public detectors meet at most two of the three, and public PII datasets are mostly English or a few European
languages (Section 2).

The bottleneck is data. Real personal data cannot be collected and redistributed for training, and human annotation in 54
languages is slow and expensive. We therefore generate the training data with large language models, but treat generation as
an engineering problem with measurable failure modes: generators copy example values, drift into English, reuse their
favourite names, ignore country formats and occasionally mislabel. Each was measured and addressed (Section 4, Appendix M).

**Contributions.**
1. A synthetic-data method for multilingual PII detection: per-country identifier formats for 79 locales, Wikidata name pools
   with foreign residents' names, explicit design axes, ~75 subtypes and two PII levels.
2. A quality-control pipeline — automatic gates, blind double review by two model families, deduplication and leakage
   analysis — with measured change rates and every generation cost.
3. Browser students in four tiers (nano 2.6 MB, small 7.1 MB, medium 11.3 MB, large 15.5 MB), distilled from two private
   teachers, compared with public detectors under stated conditions, and an SDK that runs them in the browser.
4. newmean-pii-dataset: train and validation data, a reviewed public test in 54 languages (17,058 rows) and a private test of the same design; a leaderboard reports scores on both.

## 2. Related work

A fuller version of this section, with every figure, is in Appendix M14.

**PII detection systems.** Microsoft Presidio combines NER, regular expressions, rules and checksums [1] but ships English
recognizers by default; other languages need their own models and context words [2]. GLiNER extracts entity types given as
labels [3]; its PII fine-tunes [4, 7] and GLiNER2 [5] derivatives include GLiNER2-PII (205M parameters), which covers seven
European languages and has not been measured on non-European scripts [6]. OpenAI's Privacy Filter (1.5B parameters, 50M active) is "primarily English"
[8], and an independent evaluation finds F1 below 0.04 on Persian and Ukrainian general-NER sets [9]. Browser-sized models —
Mozilla's smart-pii-redactor (14.8 MB at 4 bits) [10], Rampart (14.7 MB) [11] and Rampart Edge (1.45 MB) [12] — cover
Latin-script or unlisted languages; Horizon's pii-redactor-small (141M) [13] reports an entity F1 of 0.971 on OpenPII
validation but a redaction F1 of 0.351 on an external benchmark. Commercial APIs offer many country-specific types [14] or few
languages [15], and recent benchmarks find that aggregate scores hide failures [16, 17, 18]. The public models we found under
20 MB document Latin-script-only or unlisted language coverage; the broader multilingual ones have 100M+ parameters.

**PII datasets.** The public synthetic sets [19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24] are mostly English or European; KDPII is Korean dialogue
[25, 26]. Older multilingual NER corpora annotate generic entities, not PII [27, 28], and de-identification corpora are English
and domain-specific [29, 30]. Our data differs in language count (54), per-country identifier formats, name pools and PII
levels.

**LLM-generated training data.** LLMs cut labelling cost [31] and can generate [32] or distil [33, 56] NER training data, but
simple prompts give biased, low-diversity data while attributed prompts help [34]; synthetic data helps less with subjective
labels [35], and LLM labels are noisy [36]. We use explicit design axes and measure label errors by blind double review.

**Compression and evaluation.** Our models combine existing techniques — multilingual encoders [37, 38, 39, 40, 41],
distillation [42, 43, 44], vocabulary reduction [45, 46], 4-bit quantization [47, 48] and ONNX Runtime Web [49]. Train–test
overlap and unseen entities affect scores [50, 51, 52, 53], so we report test rows with unseen values separately; the random seed
alone shifts NER F1 by about a point [54, 55]. Span metrics do not show whether a masked text still identifies a person [57].

## 3. Task and labels

**Task.** Given a text, mark the character spans that identify or help identify a person, with a label. The SDK masks the model's
spans. The model learns structured types (e-mail, cards, IBAN, IP addresses …) as well, so it does not depend on patterns; an
optional pattern layer exists in the SDK but is off by default, because on the public test it adds almost no coverage
and doubles over-masking (Section 6).

**Labels.** Thirteen model labels: PERSON, EMAIL, PHONE, ADDRESS, ID_NUMBER, FINANCIAL, ORG, USERNAME, URL, DATE_OF_BIRTH,
SECRET, plus PERSON_L2 and URL_L2 for level-2 spans (IP addresses are learned as ID_NUMBER). The data also carries a finer
**subtype** on every span (e.g. ID_NUMBER:passport, FINANCIAL:iban; ~75 in total) and flags for notation such as masked or
spelled-out values, which allow selective masking; the model predicts labels only (Appendix M1).

**PII levels.** A full name or a passport number identifies a person on its own; a surname with a job title ("Kim, the
manager"), a nickname, initials or a link to a company's public website does so only with context. Every span gets a level,
fixed by its subtype. The levels are a masking policy — which spans a user may choose to leave visible — not a general
classification of identifiability, which depends on context:

| level | spans |
|---|---|
| 1 — direct | full names, given names, contact details, all identifier numbers, financial data, personal URLs, secrets, dates of birth, addresses, organisations a person belongs to |
| 2 — indirect | PERSON: surname (family name alone, usually with a title or honorific), alias (nickname or pet name used as a name), initials; URL: public (a link that does not point to a person) |

The SDK masks both levels by default and can keep level 2 visible. Levels emerged from error analysis: the spans on which our
earlier models were least stable across training runs were exactly nicknames and surname-plus-title mentions (Appendix I.3).

**Guideline.** A single English guideline is used verbatim in generation and review prompts. It defines what to tag per label,
span boundaries (names exclude honorifics and grammatical endings), overlap rules and culture-specific rules found during
review; it went through eleven versions (Appendix M1).

## 4. The v1.0 data

The generator code is not released (see Competing interests). Each stage is summarised below with its key figure; Appendix M
describes the method in enough detail to judge it without the code.

### 4.1 Generation

- **Design axes.** Each call asks one model for 40 items in one language and country. A planner allocates, by exact quotas,
  countries (79 locales for 54 languages), 16 domains, 20 formats (chat, e-mail, form, CSV row, log line, OCR text, …), length,
  voice, PII count, a twist (spelled-out values, code-switching, hard negatives, …) and a required focus subtype, so rare types
  appear in sufficient numbers. Details: M2.
- **PII-free items.** 40% of items contain no PII by design, because real input often has none and a model that rarely sees
  such text over-masks. 53% of them get their own twist (look-alike numbers, people named only by role, placeholders). M3.
- **Country formats.** A format card per locale, researched from official sources, gives the shapes of local identifiers.
  Values must match the format, not the checksum, which lowers the chance of matching a real person's number. The format check
  fails 0.5% of checkable validation values, and those rows stay out of training. M4.
- **Names.** Language models reuse a few names, and Faker [60] covers only part of our locales. We sample from Wikidata [58]
  name pools for all 79 locales and add foreign residents' names weighted by UN migration statistics [59]. A full name recurs
  in another call for a median 1.4% of full-name spans per language. M5.
- **Generators.** Three Claude models write the data; training and test come from different generators, so the benchmark does
  not reward the training generator's style:

  | generator | role in v1.0 | cost per call (40 rows, Message Batches) |
  |---|---|---|
  | Claude Haiku 5.5, effort high | 90% of training and validation data | $0.0104 |
  | Claude Sonnet 5.5, effort high | 10% of training and validation data, 50% of the test sets, every SECRET value in the tests | $0.098 |
  | Claude Opus 5.5, effort high | 50% of the test sets | ≈ $0.2 |

  Opus refuses to write secrets, so SECRET items go to Sonnet. Items come back with inline tags, which a strict parser turns
  into character offsets. M6.
- **Generation at scale.** Full rounds use the Message Batches API (50% discount). Every input that shapes the rows is frozen
  by hash, and every prompt and raw response is stored, so rows can be rebuilt without new API calls (the planner reproduces
  all 53,950 validation item specs exactly). M7.
- **Post-generation value rewrite.** Generators collapse onto favourite values where no natural pool exists (one card-expiry
  date made up over a third of all expiry spans). Expiry, CVV, one-time-code and respondent-code digits are rewritten
  deterministically per row, and ordinary dates shift together unless that would contradict the text (e.g. a weekday next to
  a date). Afterwards no rewritten type has a value above 3.3% of its spans. M8.

### 4.2 Quality control

- **Automatic gates.** Every round is checked for the item specs (required subtype present ≥ 95%), format validity (≤ 1%
  failures), off-language text (≤ 0.5%) and copied example values; failing rows stay out of training until resolved. M9.
- **Blind double review.** Two reviewers from different model families — Claude Opus and GPT-6.1 Sol — re-tag sampled rows
  from the raw text only, without seeing the generated labels or each other. A row counts as *changed* when both agree with
  each other and differ from the generator: an agreed-change rate, not an error rate, since it misses errors both reviewers
  share and can include wrong changes. Results on random v1.0 validation rows (sampling and definitions: M10):

  | sample | rows changed by both reviewers | of these, already held by the gates | error rate of rows that enter training |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 300 rows (Haiku 90% / Sonnet 10%) | 10 (3.3%) | 2 | 8 / 298 (2.7%; 95% interval ≈ 1.4–5.2%) |
  | 100 Sonnet rows | 5 (5%) | 3 (all three missed names) | 2 / 97 (2.1%) |
  | 100 Chinese rows, GLM vs generator | boundaries 100%, labels and levels 98.9%, subtypes 95.7% | | 1 clear generator error |

  For Chinese, GLM (Z.ai) gives a third, reference-only opinion.
- **Public test review.** Every one of the 17,058 rows was re-tagged blind by Claude Opus. It reproduced the
  generator's labels on 16,730 rows (98.1%); there two independent annotations agree and the row passes. GPT-6.1 Sol re-tagged
  blind the 328 rows where Opus differed, and also 4,400 matching rows in an early full pass. Both reviewers agreed on a label
  change in 220 rows (1.3%), and the generator's labels held by majority in 181 (83 of the 328, and 98
  matching rows from the early pass). The 25 open rows (all three versions
  differed, or a reviewer gave no answer) were adjudicated: GLM through ZCode chose among the versions,
  and Claude checked each choice against the guideline (23 kept, 2 overridden). In all, 318 rows (1.9%) changed, including
  subtype-only fixes.
- **Private test review.** The same procedure, except that open rows were adjudicated by Claude and checked by GPT
  instead of GLM, so that the private rows reach only these two model providers. Opus reproduced the generator's labels on
  16,677 of 17,001 rows (98.1%); of the 324 rows GPT-6.1 Sol then re-tagged, both agreed on a change in 201, the generator held
  by majority in 93, and 30 were open. Opus and GPT agreed on 20 of the open rows; the other 10 were decided by the human
  author after a further Claude review against the guideline and the conventions of the training data. In all, 223 rows (1.3%) changed.
- **Duplicates and leakage.** Exact and near-duplicate texts (MinHash over masked word 5-grams, Jaccard ≥ 0.7) are removed
  from the lower-priority split, never from train. Validation and the first training shard share no near-duplicate templates
  (35 exact duplicates were removed from validation), but values recur: 44% of validation PERSON values and 23% of all spans
  also occur in the training data (public test: 36% and 20%). We therefore also score the public-test rows whose PII
  values are all unseen in train (73% of the rows with PII): our students miss 1.5 / 2.0 / 2.6 / 3.2% of PII characters there
  (large to nano), against 1.6 / 2.1 / 2.7 / 3.3% on all rows, so recurring values do not raise the scores. M11.
- **Diversity.** Distinct word 3-gram ratios are about 0.95 for PII-free and PII items alike, in every training shard
  (0.948–0.957 and 0.953–0.961). M12.

### 4.4 The dataset

The data is named newmean-pii-dataset, with four splits: train, validation, public test and private test. This is v1.0
(tag `data-v1.0`). Validation and the first eighth of the training data were generated with the previous prompt
version and post-processed with the v1.0 rewrite; the final prompt differs only in a few guideline lines and one placement
rule (M13).

| split | generator | rows (approved, deduplicated) | status |
|---|---|---|---|
| train | Haiku 90% / Sonnet 10% | 1,069,002 | done |
| validation | Haiku 90% / Sonnet 10% | 53,444 (≈ 1,000 per language) | done |
| public test | Opus 50% / Sonnet 50% (SECRET items from Sonnet only) | 17,058 (≈ 320 per language) | reviewed, every row (Section 4.2) |
| private test | same, own private seed | 17,001 | reviewed, every row (Section 4.2); scored only by the leaderboard job (kept private) |

**How the splits are made.** The four splits are not cut from one pool: each is generated by its own calls, with its own seed
for the design axes and value seeds, and the two tests by different models than train and validation (Opus and Sonnet instead
of mostly Haiku), so a test template is never a sibling of a training template by construction. Rows that fail the automatic
gates are held out. The splits are then deduplicated in priority order — train, validation, private test, public test — exactly
and by near-duplicate templates; a row is only ever removed from the lower-priority split (M11). Ranking the private test above
the public one keeps any hidden row from being published as a public-test duplicate. Removed: 107 validation rows, 16 public-test
rows and 15 hidden-test rows.

**What a row is.** A row is one text item as a person would type, paste or upload it — a spreadsheet cell, a form or survey
answer, a chat thread, an e-mail, a log line, an OCR excerpt — not one sentence. On validation the median row has 167
characters (10th–90th percentile 45–419), 32% of rows span several lines, and 40% contain no PII. In total v1.0 holds
1,156,505 rows, 242M characters and 1.09M PII spans (train 223.5M characters and 1,007,176 spans, validation 11.3M and 50,502,
public test 3.6M and 17,147, private test 3.6M and 17,275).

The models learn the 13 labels of Section 3; level-2 spans make up 7.7% of the training spans (M13).

## 5. Models

**Teachers (private).** mDeBERTa-v3-base (278M parameters, 5 epochs) and XLM-R-large (559M, 2 epochs), fine-tuned on the 1,069,002
training rows of v1.0. They are used only to label soft targets for the students and are not released.

**Students (released).** All four start from mMiniLMv2-L6-H384 with the XLM-R vocabulary pruned to the 35,298 pieces that occur
in the training data, are compressed per tier and distilled for 2 epochs from the averaged soft labels of both teachers
(α 0.5, T 2). They are exported to ONNX with 4-bit weights; `q4e` also stores the embeddings in 4 bits.

| tier | structure | parameters | download (default file) |
|---|---|---|---|
| large | 6 layers, full pruned embeddings | 24.4M | 15.5 MB (q4e) |
| medium (SDK default) | 6 layers, embeddings rank 192 | 17.7M | 11.3 MB (q4e) |
| small | 4 layers, rank 64 | 9.6M | 7.1 MB (q4) |
| nano | one shared layer applied 4 times, rank 32 | 3.1M | 2.6 MB (q4) |

On the public test the 4-bit embeddings change the miss rate by at most 0.1 points for large and medium and save
2.7–5.2 MB; for small and nano they save under 1 MB and cost 0.2–0.3 points, so those two default to `q4`. One decision threshold (a bias on the "outside" logit,
exposed as `sensitivity`) trades misses for over-masking without a second model (Appendix O.1–O.2).

**In the browser.** The SDK `@newmean-ai/pii` runs the model in WebAssembly with one of two engines that read the same ONNX file:
onnxruntime-web, or a built-in engine written for these four models (freestanding C compiled to WebAssembly with SIMD; a second
build uses fused multiply-add where the browser supports relaxed SIMD). Both return the same spans as the Python evaluator on a
412-text parity set (nano q4: 410, the same two texts for both engines, from merging overlapping spans in the shared decoder).
The runtime download besides the model and tokenizer, and latency in headless Chrome (one thread; medium q4e, Apple M5 Pro):

## 6. Evaluation

The main metric is the **character miss rate**: the share of non-space PII characters left unmasked, which is the
privacy-relevant number; we also report over-masking (share of non-PII characters masked) and, in Appendix O, exact F1 (same
start, end and label). Public detectors run with their recommended settings through our adapters; they are scored against gold
collapsed to their own labels (level 2 → level 1 labels, secret-like labels → SECRET), and miss and over-masking do not depend on
labels. All numbers are model only (no regex layer) and single runs; seed-to-seed differences of our students are 0.2–0.6 points.
Everything was run on Colab L4 VMs: our students and the q4 ONNX detectors on the VM's CPU (Xeon 2.20 GHz, onnxruntime), the
transformer detectors on the L4 GPU.

**Our own sets.** Checkpoints and settings are chosen on validation only; the public test is scored once the models are fixed.
The private test is kept for the leaderboard, a Hugging Face Space that reports both: the public test per label
and language, the private test as one overall score per model, scored weekly by a Hugging Face Job (L4). Its first run
(2026-10-31, all 13 rows of the table below) put every model within about one point of its public miss rate: ours 1.5 / 2.0 /
2.4 / 3.0% (large to nano; public 1.6 / 2.1 / 2.7 / 3.3%), the public detectors between −0.4 and +1.0 points. The public
set therefore did not favour our models over a set nobody had seen.

## 7. Limitations

- **Single runs, few machines.** Model numbers are single runs (seed spread 0.2–0.6 points); phone latency comes from four
  phones (one iPhone, three Samsung Android phones), averaged.
- **Synthetic–real gap.** All training data is generated; external sets show what transfers, but real user text is messier.
- **Label conventions differ across datasets** (URLs, secrets, organisations, partial dates), so external numbers mix model
  errors with convention differences; we report per-label numbers where conventions diverge.
- **Run-to-run variance** of 1.5–2 points on external sets (Appendix I.3); single-run gaps of that size are not established.
- **Name pools** come from Wikidata and lean towards public figures; Khmer female names and a few origin pools are thin.
- **Test-set SECRET spans are written by one generator** (Sonnet), because Opus refuses to write secrets.
- **Review covers labels, not values.** The audits check spans and labels; they do not check whether a value is realistic or
  whether it belongs to a real person. Change rates come from samples of 100–300 rows and miss errors both reviewers share.
- **PII levels are a policy.** Whether a surname or a nickname identifies someone depends on context; level 2 marks spans a user
  may choose to keep visible, not spans that are safe.
- **Masking is never 100%.** We publish miss rates per language and label instead of promises.

## 8. Ethics and release

**Fake values.** Generated values may coincide with real ones. The prompts offer seed values from reserved e-mail domains
(RFC 2606), fictional telephone ranges for countries that reserve them and checksum-invalid Korean resident numbers, and the
guideline asks for format-valid, fictional identifiers; but generation does not check this. After release we scanned every
value of the public test, validation and training data and replaced those that could match real ones: national ID, payment card
and IBAN numbers whose check digits are valid (one digit changed), free-mail e-mail addresses (moved to reserved domains) and
names of public figures (public test 1.1: 569 rows; validation 1,923; training 39,724). We still do not claim that every value is
fictional, and will remove any value on request (hoonjae@newmean.ai).

**Masking.** The SDK masks the spans it detects before text is sent; anything it misses is sent unmasked (Section 7).

**Release.** Models and SDK under Apache-2.0, with a request (not a condition) to credit "Powered by newmean.ai": the SDK as
